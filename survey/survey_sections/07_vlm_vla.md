## Vision-language and vision-language-action models

By 2020 the Transformer had absorbed language modelling, and the open question was whether the same architecture and the same recipe of scale plus weak supervision could cover pixels, then pictures paired with words, and finally robot motor commands. This chapter follows that sequence as four moves. First, images were made into token sequences. Second, natural language was made the interface to vision. Third, a large language model was given eyes. Fourth, the resulting vision-language model was made to act. Each move solved one blocking problem and exposed the next.

### Images as token sequences

Convolutional networks still dominated large-scale recognition in 2020, and earlier attention-in-vision work either bolted attention onto CNNs or used specialised local, sparse or axial patterns that had "not yet been scaled effectively on modern hardware accelerators" [@vit2020]. The obstacle was an architectural mismatch: a Transformer needs a sequence, and per-pixel attention is quadratic in pixel count, so it does not scale to realistic image sizes.

The idea in [@vit2020] is to cut the image into $P\times P$ patches, flatten and linearly project each one, and feed the result to an unmodified pre-norm Transformer encoder:

$$z_0=[x_{class};x_p^1E;\dots;x_p^NE]+E_{pos},\qquad z'_\ell=\mathrm{MSA}(\mathrm{LN}(z_{\ell-1}))+z_{\ell-1},\quad z_\ell=\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{LN}(z'_\ell))+z'_\ell$$

Here $x_p^i$ is the $i$-th flattened patch, $E$ the trainable patch projection, $E_{pos}$ learned position embeddings, and $N=HW/P^2$ the sequence length. Patches become tokens, and only the patch extraction and the interpolation of position embeddings at higher resolution inject any 2D structure by hand. The architecture deliberately drops the CNN priors of locality and translation equivariance. The paper's finding is that this costs accuracy on small data (ResNets win when pre-training on ImageNet or a 9M-image JFT subset) but that "large scale training trumps inductive bias": with 90M or more pre-training images ViT wins [@vit2020].

The headline result is ViT-H/14 pre-trained on JFT-300M reaching 88.55 top-1 on ImageNet and 77.63 on the 19-task VTAB suite, against 87.54 and 76.29 for BiT-L, at 2.5k TPUv3-core-days versus 9.9k for BiT-L [@vit2020]. The authors also note that compute comparisons may be affected by schedule and optimiser, not only architecture. What this exposed, in hindsight, was a practical dependence on proprietary JFT-300M and a need for still larger and less curated supervision. Labelled categories were the bottleneck.

### Language as the interface to vision

Vision models of the time were trained to predict a fixed label set, so each new concept needed new labelled data. Earlier attempts to learn from image-text pairs performed poorly: Visual N-Grams reached 11.5% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy, and a word-by-word captioning baseline scaled badly [@clip2021].

[@clip2021] avoids generating the caption and only asks which caption belongs to which image. Over a batch of $N$ pairs, with L2-normalised embeddings $\hat I_i,\hat T_j$ and learned temperature $t$, the logits are $s_{ij}=e^{t}\hat I_i\!\cdot\!\hat T_j$ and the loss is a symmetric cross-entropy over rows and columns of the similarity matrix:

$$\mathcal{L}=\tfrac12\Big(\tfrac1N\sum_{i}-\log\frac{e^{s_{ii}}}{\sum_j e^{s_{ij}}}+\tfrac1N\sum_{j}-\log\frac{e^{s_{jj}}}{\sum_i e^{s_{ij}}}\Big)$$

This maximises similarity of the $N$ true pairs and minimises it for the $N^2-N$ mismatched ones. The formula is the note's rendering of the paper's pseudocode, not a numbered equation of the paper. The paper motivates the contrastive target as an easier proxy than predicting exact words: a Transformer caption model learned ImageNet zero-shot 3x slower than a bag-of-words predictor, and the contrastive swap added a further 4x efficiency [@clip2021].

At test time class names written as prompts ("A photo of a {label}.") pass through the text encoder, and the resulting embeddings act as the weights of a linear classifier. The paper observes that the text encoder is in effect a hypernetwork generating those weights. Trained on 400M web pairs with a ViT or modified ResNet image encoder, CLIP reaches 76.2% zero-shot ImageNet, matching the original ResNet-50 without its 1.28M labelled images, and beats a supervised ResNet-50 linear probe on 16 of 27 datasets [@clip2021]. Zero-shot accuracy under natural distribution shift also degraded much less (for example ImageNet-A 77.1 versus 2.7 for a ResNet-101 at equal ImageNet accuracy) [@clip2021].

The new problem was that CLIP can only output a similarity score over a given set of classes. It cannot caption, answer questions or follow instructions. The authors also say zero-shot CLIP is only on average competitive with a ResNet-50 linear probe, well below overall state of the art, and estimate that roughly 1000x more compute would be needed to close the gap [@clip2021].

### Giving a language model eyes

Two papers then connected a strong vision encoder to a strong language model in opposite ways.

[@flamingo2022] wanted GPT-3-style few-shot prompting with images and free-form text output. It keeps a contrastively pre-trained vision encoder and a 70B Chinchilla language model frozen and adds trainable glue: a Perceiver Resampler that compresses visual features to 64 tokens, and gated cross-attention layers inserted between language-model blocks:

$$y \leftarrow y+\tanh(\alpha_{xattn})\,\mathrm{attention}(q{=}y,\,kv{=}x),\qquad y\leftarrow y+\tanh(\alpha_{dense})\,\mathrm{ffw}(y)$$

Each $\alpha$ is a learnable scalar initialised to 0, so at initialisation the model equals the pretrained language model and vision flows in gradually. Removing the gate lowered the Flamingo-3B overall ablation score by 4.2% and caused instabilities [@flamingo2022]. Training maximises the autoregressive likelihood $p(y|x)=\prod_{\ell}p(y_\ell\mid y_{<\ell},x_{\le\ell})$, in which each text token sees only the images that precede it. Because training sequences interleave images and text (web pages, image-caption pairs and video-caption pairs), a few (image, answer) examples in the prompt adapt the model with no weight updates. On 16 benchmarks Flamingo beat prior zero/few-shot methods by a large margin; with 32 examples it beat the fine-tuned state of the art on six tasks according to the text, while the Table 1 caption says seven [@flamingo2022]. It did not win everywhere: on COCO captioning the 32-shot score was 113.8 versus 143.3 for the fine-tuned state of the art [@flamingo2022]. The ablations also show that fine-tuning the language model instead of freezing it hurt (62.7 versus 70.7 overall), which the authors attribute to catastrophic forgetting [@flamingo2022].

[@llava2023] attacked a different bottleneck: there was no vision-language instruction data. It had text-only GPT-4 write conversations, detailed descriptions and reasoning questions from COCO captions and bounding boxes, yielding 158K samples (58K, 23K and 77K by type). The model is a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14, one trainable linear layer, and Vicuna:

$$H_v=W\cdot Z_v,\quad Z_v=g(X_v),\qquad p(X_a\mid X_v,X_{instruct})=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p_\theta\big(x_i\mid X_v,X_{instruct,<i},X_{a,<i}\big)$$

The image features $Z_v$ become visual tokens $H_v$ that look like word embeddings, and the usual language-model loss is computed only on the assistant's answer tokens. Training has two stages: the projection alone on 595K filtered CC3M pairs, then projection plus LLM on the instruction data [@llava2023]. Results include 85.1% relative score on a 30-image COCO benchmark (21.5% without instruction tuning), 67.3 on an in-the-wild set versus 38.1 for BLIP-2 and 19.1 for OpenFlamingo, and 90.92% on ScienceQA against 91.68% for the previous best [@llava2023]. The judge is GPT-4 comparing against a text-only GPT-4 that sees ground-truth captions and boxes, and the benchmarks are small (90 and 60 questions) [@llava2023].

Taken together the two papers separate the design space. Flamingo needs a 1536-TPU, 15-day training run, and neither the 80B model nor its data were released [@flamingo2022]. LLaVA trains on 8 A100s with a single linear connector and released its data, code and checkpoints [@llava2023]. The LLaVA authors explicitly left fancier connectors such as gated cross-attention for future work, and later versions of the recipe changed the connector and data [@llava2023]. In hindsight the simple projector became the template for the open "encoder, projector, LLM" family.

### From describing the world to acting in it

A vision-language model can answer questions but a robot needs motor commands. Earlier uses of language models in robotics mostly sat at the planning level, calling low-level controllers that never benefited from web knowledge, and robot data was tiny by comparison with web data [@rt2_2023].

[@rt2_2023] writes actions as text. Each continuous action dimension is discretised into 256 bins, so a step becomes a string of eight integers (terminate flag, six end-effector deltas, gripper). The note's simplified rendering of the binning is $b_k=\mathrm{round}\big(255\,(a_k-a_k^{min})/(a_k^{max}-a_k^{min})\big)$. The model is co-fine-tuned on robot trajectories and the original web vision-language data with ordinary next-token cross-entropy:

$$\min_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim \mathcal{D}_{robot}\cup\mathcal{D}_{web}}\Big[-\sum_\ell \log p_\theta(y_\ell\mid y_{<\ell},x)\Big]$$

Here $x$ is an image plus prompt and $y$ is either language or action tokens. The paper gives only the string format and the bin count, so this formalisation is the note's, not the paper's. No new layers are added, and decoding is constrained to valid action tokens when an action is requested. On the PaLI-X (5B, 55B) and PaLM-E (12B) backbones, seen-task success matched RT-1 (91 and 93 versus 92), while unseen-generalisation average was 62 for both RT-2 variants versus 32 for RT-1 [@rt2_2023]. The ablation shows that pretraining, co-fine-tuning and scale each matter: unseen average was 9 for a 5B model from scratch, 42 fine-tuned, 44 co-fine-tuned, 52 for 55B fine-tuned and 63 for 55B co-fine-tuned [@rt2_2023]. The same paper reports 62 for this model elsewhere (a rounding or run difference in the source).

Two obstacles then appeared. First, RT-2 was closed. [@openvla2024] supplied an open recipe: a Prismatic-7B backbone (fused SigLIP and DINOv2 encoders, an MLP projector, Llama 2 7B) fine-tuned with the same action-as-token objective $\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{k}\log p_\theta(b_k\mid I,\ell,b_{<k})\big]$ on 970k Open X-Embodiment episodes. It changes the binning to the 1st and 99th data quantiles so outliers do not stretch the bins. On a BridgeData V2 WidowX evaluation OpenVLA scored 70.6% against 50.6% for RT-2-X, and on the Google robot 85.0% against 78.3%, which the paper treats as comparable; RT-2-X was better at semantic generalisation, which the authors attribute to its web co-training [@openvla2024]. LoRA at rank 32 matched full fine-tuning (68.2% versus 69.7%) with about 1.4% of the parameters, and 4-bit serving matched bfloat16 (71.9% versus 71.3%) [@openvla2024]. Those two ablations used a smaller SigLIP-only variant, not the final model.

Second, 256-bin text tokens are coarse and slow for high-frequency dexterous work, which [@pi0_2024] names as a major challenge. It pairs a 3B PaliGemma VLM with a roughly 300M-parameter action expert that generates a chunk of $H=50$ continuous actions by flow matching:

$$L^{\tau}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\big\|v_\theta(A_t^\tau,o_t)-u(A_t^\tau|A_t)\big\|^2,\qquad A_t^\tau=\tau A_t+(1-\tau)\epsilon,\quad u=A_t-\epsilon$$

Here $\tau\in[0,1]$ is flow time (noise at 0, data at 1), $o_t$ the images, language and joint angles, and $v_\theta$ the action expert. Along the straight noise-to-data path the target velocity is constant, so a network trained to predict it can be integrated with 10 Euler steps, $A_t^{\tau+\delta}=A_t^\tau+\delta v_\theta(A_t^\tau,o_t)$ with $\delta=0.1$, to turn noise into an action chunk [@pi0_2024]. The paper reports 73 ms on-board inference on an RTX 4090 and chunks executed open-loop at up to 50 Hz [@pi0_2024]. Pre-training uses about 10,000 hours of data over 7 robot configurations and 68 tasks, then post-training on a small curated set per task. In the paper's bar charts pi0 beats OpenVLA and Octo by a large margin and exceeds 50% of the maximum score on multi-stage tasks such as laundry folding, but the text supplies no per-task numbers [@pi0_2024].

### What this line got wrong or left open

The authors state several limits directly. Zero-shot CLIP is far from state of the art on tasks outside its web distribution [@clip2021]. Flamingo inherits language-model hallucination and its in-context gains scale poorly with the number of shots [@flamingo2022]. LLaVA's single linear connector yields a bag-of-patches view that fails on fine detail, and the authors say it needs higher-resolution input [@llava2023]. RT-2's robot gains no new physical skills from web data, and failures cluster on grasping by specific parts, novel motions and dexterous tasks [@rt2_2023]. OpenVLA supports one image only, cannot serve 50 Hz setups, and usually stays below 90% success [@openvla2024]. For pi0 the authors concede that how to weight the pre-training data, how much data gives near-perfect performance, and whether the recipe extends to driving or legged locomotion are all unknown [@pi0_2024].

Several problems are visible mainly in hindsight, and the notes mark them as such. Reproducibility was uneven: JFT-300M, WIT, the Flamingo model and data, the RT-2 models, and the pi0 pre-training set were all proprietary, so open reproductions came later (the LAION-based ones for CLIP, for example) [@vit2020; @clip2021; @flamingo2022; @rt2_2023; @pi0_2024]. Evaluation is thin in the robotics half: OpenVLA's comparisons use 170 and 60 rollouts with wide standard errors, and pi0 reports 10 trials per task in bar charts [@openvla2024; @pi0_2024]. The comparison between OpenVLA and pi0 is not clean, since the OpenVLA and Octo baselines were trained on a reduced schedule and not designed for chunking, as the pi0 authors acknowledge [@pi0_2024]. Likewise pi0's language-following advantage over a non-VLM variant is confounded by model size [@pi0_2024].

Finally, whether web co-training is what produces semantic generalisation in robots is suggested but not isolated. RT-2 ablates it (44 co-fine-tuned versus 42 fine-tuned at 5B) and the OpenVLA authors infer it from RT-2-X's semantic edge, yet OpenVLA lists it as an open design question [@rt2_2023; @openvla2024]. The discrete-token approach of RT-2 and OpenVLA gave way to continuous flow-matched chunks in pi0, so how much of the VLA gain comes from the language-model backbone and how much from the action representation remains open.

### Hand-off to the next chapter

This chapter supplied three ingredients that later chapters reuse. The patch-token view of images, together with contrastive and instruction-tuned vision-language pre-training, gave a shared visual encoder and language interface. The VLA recipe of a pretrained VLM acting through a policy head gave robotics a way to inherit web knowledge. The flow-matching action expert in [@pi0_2024] borrows its objective from the generative-modelling line, which Chapter 6 covered. In hindsight, the same patch-token Transformer also underlies latent-diffusion and predictive world-model work.
