## World models

Model-free reinforcement learning pays for every skill in environment interaction, and the earlier chapters of this survey show how large that bill became. A world model is the attempt to pay it with computation instead: learn how the environment responds to actions, then use the learned response to plan, to train a policy, or to generate experience. This chapter follows that idea from 2018 to 2025. It also separates the papers that make a world-model claim from those that are better described as general representation learning or video generation, because the set labelled "world models" in the literature mixes the two.

### From a dream to a latent state-space model

The starting point is a model that is trained without reward and used for control. The agent in [@worldmodels2018] has a vision module (a VAE that compresses each frame to a latent $z_t$), a memory module (an LSTM with a mixture-density output that predicts $z_{t+1}$ from $(a_t,z_t,h_t)$), and a controller that is a single linear layer, $a_t=W_c[z_t\ h_t]+b_c$, with 867 parameters on CarRacing and 1,088 on Doom. The controller is optimised with CMA-ES because it has so few parameters. Because the memory module outputs a distribution, it can be sampled to produce a hallucinated environment. A controller trained only inside that dream scored 1092 ± 556 in the real VizDoom Take Cover task (solve threshold 750), but the temperature of the sampling had to be tuned, since the controller exploited imperfections of a low-temperature model (virtual score 2086 against real score 193 at $\tau=0.1$) [@worldmodels2018].

The three modules were trained in separate stages, and the evolution strategy gave no gradient through the model. Both facts blocked scaling, and the authors list the first as a limitation. The next paper in the line, [@planet2018], addressed the model itself. Its Recurrent State-Space Model (RSSM) splits the latent state into a deterministic recurrent part $h_t$ and a stochastic part $s_t$:

$$h_t=f(h_{t-1},s_{t-1},a_{t-1}),\quad s_t\sim p(s_t\mid h_t),\quad o_t\sim p(o_t\mid h_t,s_t),\quad r_t\sim p(r_t\mid h_t,s_t)$$

The deterministic path carries information across many steps, and the stochastic path represents uncertainty and multiple futures. The ablation in [@planet2018] found that both paths were needed, and that the purely stochastic path mattered more, since the agent did not learn without it. The model is trained with a variational bound on the observation likelihood plus reward terms, with 3 free nats of KL. PlaNet used no policy or value network. At each step it ran the cross-entropy method over action sequences inside the latent model (horizon 12, 1000 candidates, 10 iterations) and executed the first action. On six DeepMind Control tasks from 64x64 pixels it used 1,000 episodes where D4PG used 100,000, which the paper summarises as about 200 times fewer on average, while its final scores were mixed relative to D4PG (for example 700 against 985 on finger spin, 662 against 524 on cheetah) [@planet2018]. PlaNet also proposed "latent overshooting" for multi-step prediction, but the authors report that it slightly lowered RSSM performance and dropped it from the final agent [@planet2018].

### Where the model is used: short rollouts, imagination and search

Once a model exists, the question becomes how to turn it into behaviour without letting the policy exploit the model's errors. Three answers appeared within about a year, and they are different enough to be worth separating.

The first answer is to use the model only briefly. [@mbpo2019] works on state-based MuJoCo tasks, with no pixels. It branches short rollouts of $k$ steps from states that were actually visited in the real replay buffer and feeds the synthetic transitions to SAC. Its Theorem 4.2 bounds the true return by the branched-rollout return minus an error term:

$$\eta[\pi]\ge\eta^{branch}[\pi]-2r_{\max}\Big[\frac{\gamma^{k+1}\epsilon_\pi}{(1-\gamma)^2}+\frac{\gamma^k+2}{1-\gamma}\epsilon_\pi+\frac{k}{1-\gamma}(\epsilon_m+2\epsilon_\pi)\Big]$$

Here $\epsilon_m$ is model error on the data-collecting policy's states and $\epsilon_\pi$ is policy shift. Read literally, the bound is maximised at $k=0$, that is, it argues against using a model at all. The authors then measure that model error on the current policy grows roughly linearly with policy shift (Theorem 4.3), after which a nonzero $k$ is optimal. The empirical result is that MBPO matches the asymptotic performance of SAC and PPO with far fewer samples; on Ant it reaches at 300 thousand steps what SAC reaches at 3 million [@mbpo2019]. The theory is the part that is honest about its own weakness, since it needs an empirically estimated assumption to justify its headline design.

The second answer is imagination in latent space. [@simple2019] trains a pixel-level stochastic video-prediction model of Atari (discrete latent bits, scheduled sampling, rollouts of 50 steps from sampled real states) and trains PPO inside it, iterating between data collection and model fitting for 102,400 real interactions. It was the first iterated video-prediction model plus model-free learner to work on pixel Atari at 100K steps. Its claim of beating Rainbow was weakened once Rainbow was tuned for low data: the revised text says each is better in 13 of 26 games, and the advantage vanishes at about 500K samples [@simple2019]. [@dreamer2019] combines the RSSM of PlaNet with a learned actor and critic trained entirely on imagined latent trajectories. Starting from latent states encoded from replayed sequences, it imagines $H$ steps, estimates returns beyond the horizon with a value network, and updates the actor by the analytic gradient of a $\lambda$-return through the differentiable dynamics:

$$\max_\phi\ \mathbb{E}_{q_\theta,q_\phi}\sum_{\tau=t}^{t+H}V_\lambda(s_\tau),\qquad \min_\psi\ \mathbb{E}_{q_\theta,q_\phi}\sum_{\tau=t}^{t+H}\tfrac12\big(v_\psi(s_\tau)-V_\lambda(s_\tau)\big)^2$$

The first term trains the actor by pathwise gradients through reward, value, transition and action networks, which have lower variance than REINFORCE estimates; the second regresses the critic on stop-gradient targets. Acting needs no search. On 20 control tasks from pixels, Dreamer reached an average of 823 after $5\times10^6$ steps, against 332 for PlaNet and 786 for D4PG after $10^8$ steps, with about 3 hours per $10^6$ steps against 11 hours for PlaNet [@dreamer2019]. The authors state that pure world-model agents were not yet competitive on Atari and DMLab at that point.

The third answer drops reconstruction altogether. [@muzero2019] learns a hidden state with three functions, $s^0=h_\theta(o_1,\dots,o_t)$, $r^k,s^k=g_\theta(s^{k-1},a^k)$ and $\mathbf p^k,v^k=f_\theta(s^k)$, and runs Monte Carlo tree search inside it. The training loss touches only reward, value and policy:

$$l_t(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}l^r(u_{t+k},r^k_t)+l^v(z_{t+k},v^k_t)+l^p(\pi_{t+k},p^k_t)+c\|\theta\|^2$$

No term compares the hidden state with the observation, so the state may encode only what planning needs (the paper's phrase is that the agent can "invent, internally, the rules or dynamics that lead to most accurate planning"). On 57 Atari games MuZero reached a median of 2041.1% human-normalised score and matched AlphaZero in chess and shogi without being given the rules [@muzero2019]. The dynamics function is deterministic, and the authors leave stochastic transitions to future work. In hindsight, the learned model is tied to the planning procedure that trained it, so it is a value-equivalent model and not a reusable simulator. The contrast with the reconstruction-based line above is the main fork of this chapter.

### Making one world model general: discrete latents and robust losses

The Dreamer line then asked why world-model agents were not competitive on Atari. [@dreamerv2_2020] changed the stochastic latent from a diagonal Gaussian to 32 categorical variables with 32 classes each, trained with straight-through gradients, and replaced free nats with KL balancing, which trains the prior four times faster than the posterior is pulled toward it. For discrete-action Atari it also used REINFORCE for the actor ($\rho=1$) in place of dynamics backpropagation. The agent reached gamer-normalised median 2.15 on 55 games at 200M steps, against 1.47 for Rainbow and 1.29 for IQN, and the authors claim it was the first agent to reach human-level Atari by learning behaviour inside a separately trained world model. The ablations support the discrete latents (better than Gaussian on 42 of 55 tasks), but the authors write that they do not know why [@dreamerv2_2020].

The remaining obstacle was tuning: DreamerV2 used different values of $\beta$, $\rho$ and $\eta$ for Atari and control. [@dreamerv3_2023] keeps the categorical RSSM but makes losses and targets insensitive to scale. Its world-model loss has three terms, with two stop-gradient KL terms of weights 1 and 0.1 and a free-bits floor of 1 nat:

$$\mathcal L(\phi)=\mathbb E_{q_\phi}\Big[\sum_{t}\big(\beta_{pred}\mathcal L_{pred}+\beta_{dyn}\mathcal L_{dyn}+\beta_{rep}\mathcal L_{rep}\big)\Big],\quad \beta_{pred}=\beta_{dyn}=1,\ \beta_{rep}=0.1$$

On top of that come symlog transforms, $\mathrm{symlog}(x)=\mathrm{sign}(x)\ln(|x|+1)$, twohot regression for rewards and returns, and percentile return normalisation. With these, one configuration is used across 8 domains and over 150 tasks, and Dreamer agents discovered diamonds in Minecraft within 100M environment steps without human data [@dreamerv3_2023]. The authors report that performance rests mainly on the reconstruction loss and increases monotonically with model size from 12M to 400M parameters. The paper's "fixed hyperparameters" still allow a replay ratio, action repeat and model size that differ by benchmark (its Table 2), so the claim is narrower than it sounds. The year in the survey metadata (2025) and the arXiv text read (v2, 2024) differ, a bibliographic detail that does not affect the content.

### Generative simulators: world models from video

A second family treats the world model as a generative model of observations that an agent, or a person, can query. Two papers here are control-oriented world models in the strict sense, and one is a platform paper whose world-model content is thinner.

[@diamond2024] is the direct descendant of the Dreamer and SimPLe line. It replaces discrete-latent dynamics with an action- and history-conditioned diffusion model in pixel space, using the EDM formulation so that sampling is stable with 3 denoising steps. The training loss is a conditional denoising loss, $\mathcal L(\theta)=\mathbb E\|D_\theta(x^\tau_{t+1},\tau,x^0_{\le t},a_{\le t})-x^0_{t+1}\|^2$, which models the observation-space transition. A separate CNN-LSTM predicts reward and termination, and an actor-critic is trained inside the model. On Atari 100k it reached a mean human-normalised score of 1.459 and an interquartile mean of 0.641, with 11 superhuman games, against 1.266 and 0.636 for STORM, 1.097 and 0.497 for DreamerV3, and 0.332 for SimPLe [@diamond2024]. The interquartile mean only ties STORM, and the paper itself notes that search-based methods such as EfficientZero and BBF are stronger on this benchmark and not directly comparable. Its CS:GO model is a stand-alone playable generator with no RL and no quantitative metrics, so it should be read as a demonstration and not as a result about control [@diamond2024].

[@genie2024] drops action labels. A latent action model compresses what changed between consecutive frames into one of 8 discrete codes, a video tokenizer produces discrete tokens, and an autoregressive MaskGIT dynamics model predicts the next frame's tokens given the past and a latent action. At play time the user picks a code. Trained on about 30k hours of filtered platformer gameplay (a 10.1B-parameter dynamics model), it turns out-of-distribution prompts into playable scenes. The world-model-specific claim is the unsupervised action space; the scaling evidence is training-loss curves, the evaluation uses FVD and a controllability metric $\Delta_t\mathrm{PSNR}$, and agent use is limited to an imitation test on CoinRun, with no RL inside the model [@genie2024]. The authors state that it hallucinates, keeps only 16 frames of memory, and runs at around 1 FPS.

[@cosmos2025] defines a world model as a function from past observations and a control input $c_t$ to the next observation, and then builds large-scale video generation: about 100M clips from 20M hours of video, causal tokenizers, diffusion and autoregressive transformers, and post-training on robot, camera and driving data. Most of the paper (data curation, tokenizers, base-model quality, 3D consistency) is general video generation engineering. Its own physics benchmark finds that "all the WFMs equally struggle with physics adherence", and the authors state that the models "fall short as reliable simulators of the physical world". The paper also states that it contains no empirical results for policy evaluation, training or planning [@cosmos2025]. Its robotics evidence is open-loop: instruction-following human preference of 78.3% for the 7B model on 23 test episodes, and Bridge next-frame prediction at PSNR 21.14 and FVD 190 against 19.13 and 593 for IRASim-Action.

### Predicting in feature space: the JEPA papers

The last strand predicts embeddings rather than pixels. It is the weakest world-model case at its start and a stronger one at its end, and the difference should be stated plainly.

[@ijepa2023] is a self-supervised image representation paper. A context block of an image predicts the embeddings of several target blocks produced by an EMA target encoder, with an $L_2$ loss on target patches only. It reached 79.3% ImageNet linear-probe accuracy with ViT-H/14 and 73.3% with 1% of labels, and representation targets beat pixel targets (66.9 against 40.7 on 1% ImageNet) [@ijepa2023]. The "world model" connection is the motivation and the JEPA framing it cites; the paper has no environment, action, time or planning experiments. In hindsight, it contributes a training recipe for predictive abstract features, and nothing in it is a model of dynamics.

[@vjepa2_2025] is two papers in one. Sections on video pretraining, attentive-probe classification, Epic-Kitchens action anticipation and video question answering are general video representation learning: an action-free encoder trained by masked feature prediction with an $L_1$ loss on 22M videos. The world-model claim rests on its second stage, V-JEPA 2-AC, in which a frozen encoder is paired with a roughly 300M-parameter predictor trained on about 62 hours of Droid robot video with end-effector states and actions. To act, it searches by the cross-entropy method for the action sequence whose predicted features match those of a goal image:

$$\mathcal E(\hat a_{1:T};z_k,s_k,z_g)=\lVert P(\hat a_{1:T};s_k,z_k)-z_g\rVert_1,\qquad a^\star=\arg\min_{\hat a_{1:T}}\mathcal E$$

The prediction is made entirely in feature space, so nothing is rendered. On Franka arms in two labs not in Droid, with 10 trials per cell, it reached 100% on reaching, 65% and 25% on grasping a cup and a box, and 80% and 65% on pick-and-place; the behaviour-cloning baseline Octo averaged 15% on grasping the cup and 10% on pick-and-place of the box (cells identified by the table's column order in the reading note) [@vjepa2_2025]. In the authors' comparison with a fine-tuned Cosmos model it took 16 seconds per action against 4 minutes, but they disclose that the Cosmos configuration was changed for this task, so it is one setup, not a definitive comparison. Only the first stage is action-free; planning requires actions and proprioception.

### What this line got wrong or left open

Several limitations were stated by the authors. The controller in [@worldmodels2018] exploits model errors; [@mbpo2019] shows that a literal reading of its own bound argues against using the model; [@muzero2019] has deterministic dynamics; [@dreamerv2_2020] cannot explain why categorical latents help; [@genie2024] has 16 frames of memory; [@diamond2024] notes minimal frame-stacking memory and that CS:GO drifts out of distribution and forgets state near walls; and [@cosmos2025] states that its models are not reliable physics simulators. [@vjepa2_2025] notes camera sensitivity, error accumulation and the need for image sub-goals.

Other limitations appear only in hindsight. The Atari results of [@simple2019], [@dreamerv2_2020] and [@diamond2024] depend on benchmark protocols (100K steps or 200M steps, per-game training), and the claims of dominance change with tuning, as the revised [@simple2019] text shows. The Dreamer line is reconstruction-driven, so what the model represents follows from what pixels it can reconstruct; the [@dreamerv2_2020] authors suggest this as the cause of failure on Video Pinball, where the ball is a single pixel, and [@dreamerv3_2023] reports that performance rests mainly on reconstruction. In contrast, [@muzero2019] avoids reconstruction but yields a model that is useful only through its planner. Whether a prediction-in-feature-space model in the style of [@vjepa2_2025] avoids both problems at larger scale is not settled by 10-trial robot results on one embodiment family.

There is also a definitional issue the chapter should not hide. The strict reading of "world model" requires an action-conditioned transition model that is used for planning or policy learning. Under that reading, [@worldmodels2018], [@planet2018], [@simple2019], [@mbpo2019], [@dreamer2019], [@muzero2019], [@dreamerv2_2020], [@dreamerv3_2023] and [@diamond2024] qualify with experiments to match. [@genie2024] learns an action-conditioned model with latent actions but shows only an imitation test and no RL inside it. [@vjepa2_2025] qualifies only through its second stage. [@cosmos2025] defines the term but reports no empirical use of it for control, and [@ijepa2023] is representation learning with a world-model motivation. Last, none of the Atari world-model agents is evaluated on whether the learned model supports transfer to a different task; each agent is trained per environment.

### Hand-off to the next chapter

This chapter supplied three things to later work: a recipe for learning a compact latent state of the world from observations and actions (the RSSM and its discrete successors), the practice of improving a policy on imagined rollouts rather than only on real data, and a set of generative and predictive models, from diffusion to latent-action video models to feature-space predictors, that can be conditioned on actions. These matter most for the open problems in Chapter 10, where the question is whether web-scale video models can be turned into closed-loop controllers. In hindsight, the gap between the control-trained Dreamer-style models and the video-trained models is the gap that Chapter 10 takes up: the former have verified policy-learning results on narrow domains, and the latter have breadth but, in the evidence of this chapter, little demonstrated closed-loop control.
