# Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes
**Authors, venue, year:** Diederik P. Kingma, Max Welling (Univ. of Amsterdam); arXiv:1312.6114 (v11 text); ICLR 2014 as cited in `gan2014`'s reference list (not printed in this text) · **Key:** `vae2013` · **OpenAlex citations:** 45017 · **Era:** dl
**Read status:** full text of main body (Sec. 1-7); appendices (B, C: KL and MLP details) only located, not read in full

## 1. Problem and motivation
For directed models with continuous latent variables $z$ whose posterior $p_\theta(z|x)$ is intractable, EM cannot be used and mean-field variational Bayes needs intractable expectations (Sec. 1, 2.1). The naive Monte Carlo score-function gradient of the variational bound has very high variance, and sampling methods like Monte Carlo EM are too slow per datapoint on large datasets (Sec. 2.1-2.2).

## 2. Core idea in one paragraph
Train a generative model (decoder) and an approximate-posterior network (encoder) together by maximising a lower bound on the data likelihood. The trick: write the random latent sample as a deterministic function of the encoder's outputs plus independent noise, e.g. $z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon$. Then the sample is differentiable with respect to the encoder parameters, so ordinary backpropagation gives low-variance gradients of the bound. The result is an autoencoder whose code is regularised by a KL penalty toward a simple prior, and which can also generate new data by sampling the prior.

## 3. The mathematics
**Variational bound (Eq. 1-3):**
$$\log p_\theta(x^{(i)})=D_{KL}\big(q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})\,\|\,p_\theta(z|x^{(i)})\big)+\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x^{(i)}),$$
$$\mathcal L=-D_{KL}\big(q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})\|p_\theta(z)\big)+\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})}\big[\log p_\theta(x^{(i)}|z)\big]$$
| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $x^{(i)}$ | datapoint $i$ of $N$ i.i.d. samples |
| $z$ | continuous latent variable ("code") |
| $p_\theta(z),p_\theta(x|z)$ | prior and likelihood (probabilistic decoder) |
| $q_\phi(z|x)$ | recognition model (probabilistic encoder), approximates the true posterior |
| $\mathcal L$ | variational lower bound (ELBO) |

Meaning: since the KL to the true posterior is non-negative, $\mathcal L\le\log p_\theta(x)$; maximising it improves the model and the posterior approximation together. The first term regularises the code, the second is expected negative reconstruction error.

**Reparameterisation (Eq. 4-5):** $\tilde z=g_\phi(\epsilon,x)$ with $\epsilon\sim p(\epsilon)$, so $\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z|x)}[f(z)]=\mathbb E_{p(\epsilon)}[f(g_\phi(\epsilon,x))]\simeq\frac1L\sum_{l}f(g_\phi(\epsilon^{(l)},x))$. Derivation (Sec. 2.4): $q_\phi(z|x)\prod dz_i=p(\epsilon)\prod d\epsilon_i$ under the deterministic map, so the integral can be moved onto $\epsilon$, whose distribution does not depend on $\phi$. Three recipes are listed: inverse CDF, location-scale families, composition.

**SGVB estimators (Eq. 6-8):** $\tilde{\mathcal L}^A=\frac1L\sum_l[\log p_\theta(x,z^{(l)})-\log q_\phi(z^{(l)}|x)]$ and the lower-variance $\tilde{\mathcal L}^B=-D_{KL}(q_\phi\|p_\theta(z))+\frac1L\sum_l\log p_\theta(x|z^{(l)})$ when the KL is analytic; the minibatch form is $\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;X)\simeq\frac NM\sum_{i=1}^M\tilde{\mathcal L}(\theta,\phi;x^{(i)})$.

**Gaussian VAE (Eq. 9-10):** with $p_\theta(z)=\mathcal N(0,I)$, $q_\phi(z|x)=\mathcal N(\mu^{(i)},\sigma^{2(i)}I)$:
$$\mathcal L\simeq\frac12\sum_{j=1}^J\big(1+\log(\sigma_j^2)-\mu_j^2-\sigma_j^2\big)+\frac1L\sum_l\log p_\theta(x^{(i)}|z^{(i,l)}),\quad z^{(i,l)}=\mu^{(i)}+\sigma^{(i)}\odot\epsilon^{(l)}$$
$J$ is the latent dimension, $\mu,\sigma$ are encoder MLP outputs, $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. The KL term is closed form (Appendix B).

## 4. Method / architecture / algorithm
1. Sample minibatch of $M$ datapoints (M = 100) and noise $\epsilon$ (L = 1 sample per datapoint) (Alg. 1).
2. Encoder MLP outputs $\mu,\sigma$; sample $z$ by reparameterisation; decoder MLP gives Bernoulli (binary data) or Gaussian likelihood.
3. Backpropagate through the estimator; update $\theta,\phi$ jointly with SGD or Adagrad, plus small weight decay (Sec. 5).
4. Experiments: one hidden layer of 500 units (MNIST), 200 (Frey Face); parameters initialised $\mathcal N(0,0.01)$; Adagrad global stepsize from {0.01, 0.02, 0.1}.

## 5. Achievements
- Sec. 5, Fig. 2: AEVB converges faster and to a better lower bound than wake-sleep on MNIST (latent sizes 3 to 200) and Frey Face (2 to 20); more latent variables did not cause overfitting, attributed to the regularising bound. Cost 20-40 minutes per million training samples on a CPU.
- Fig. 3: estimated marginal likelihood (3 latent variables, 100 hidden units) for $N_{train}$ = 1000 and 50000: AEVB beats wake-sleep and Monte Carlo EM, which cannot be applied efficiently to full MNIST.
- Appendix A (not read): 2D latent manifold visualisations.

## 6. Limitations
**Stated by the authors:** requires continuous latent variables and differentiability (Sec. 2.4; wake-sleep also covers discrete ones, Sec. 4); experiments only with global parameters point-estimated, fully Bayesian parameters left to future work; marginal-likelihood estimates became unreliable beyond low latent dimensions (Sec. 5). Future work lists hierarchical, convolutional, time-series and supervised variants (Sec. 7).
**Seen in hindsight (hindsight):** samples tend to be blurry, partly from the factorised Gaussian posterior and likelihood; posterior collapse; only small datasets (MNIST, Frey Face) and MLPs are tested.

## 7. Place in the lineage
- **Builds on:** wake-sleep (Hinton et al. 1995), stochastic variational inference, auto-encoders; Adagrad for optimisation.
