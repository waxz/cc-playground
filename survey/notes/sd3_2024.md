# Scaling Rectified Flow Transformers for High-Resolution Image Synthesis
**Authors, venue, year:** Patrick Esser*, Sumith Kulal, Andreas Blattmann, Rahim Entezari, Jonas Müller, Harry Saini, Yam Levi, Dominik Lorenz, Axel Sauer, Frederic Boesel, Dustin Podell, Tim Dockhorn, Zion English, Kyle Lacey, Alex Goodwin, Yannik Marek, Robin Rombach* (Stability AI); arXiv:2403.03206v1, March 2024 (no conference named in the extracted text; the text is formatted as an ICML-style paper) · **Key:** `sd3_2024` · **OpenAlex citations:** 5813 · **Era:** df

## 1. Problem and motivation
Rectified flow (straight data-to-noise paths) has appealing theory and few-step sampling, but at the time of writing it was "not yet decisively established in practice": advantages had been shown mostly in small or medium, class-conditional experiments (Sec. 1). Meanwhile, text-to-image diffusion models feed a frozen text embedding into the image network through cross-attention, which the authors argue is not ideal.

## 2. Core idea in one paragraph
Combine three things and test them at scale. (1) Train with the rectified-flow objective but change how training times $t$ are sampled: draw them from a logit-normal distribution so that the hard intermediate noise levels get more training signal than the easy endpoints. (2) Use a new transformer, MM-DiT, in which image tokens and text tokens each have their own weights but are concatenated into one sequence for attention, so information flows both ways between text and image. (3) Scale the whole recipe (up to 8B parameters) and show the validation loss falls smoothly with size and tracks benchmark scores and human preference. Analogy: instead of the image "looking up" fixed text notes, two specialists (image, text) sit at one table and talk throughout.

## 3. The mathematics
Convention note: this paper puts data at $t=0$ and noise at $t=1$ (reverse of the Flow Matching paper).

**(a) Forward path and flow matching (Eqs. 2, 8, 9).**
$$z_t=a_tx_0+b_t\epsilon,\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I);\qquad \mathcal L_{CFM}=\mathbb E_{t,p_t(z|\epsilon),p(\epsilon)}\|v_\Theta(z,t)-u_t(z|\epsilon)\|_2^2,\quad u_t(z|\epsilon)=\tfrac{a_t'}{a_t}z-\epsilon b_t\Big(\tfrac{a_t'}{a_t}-\tfrac{b_t'}{b_t}\Big)$$
| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $x_0$, $\epsilon$ | data (latent) and Gaussian noise |
| $a_t,b_t$ | schedule, $a_0=1,b_0=0,a_1=0,b_1=1$ |
| $v_\Theta$ | network velocity, ODE $dy_t=v_\Theta(y_t,t)dt$ (Eq. 1) |
| $u_t(z|\epsilon)$ | conditional vector field generating $p_t(\cdot|\epsilon)$ |

Regress the velocity of the per-noise conditional path; it has the same gradients as the intractable marginal objective (Lipman et al.; paper Appendix B.1).

**(b) Unified weighted noise-prediction form (Eqs. 10-12 and the weighted loss).**
$$\mathcal L_w(x_0)=-\tfrac12\mathbb E_{t\sim\mathcal U(t),\epsilon}\big[w_t\lambda_t'\|\epsilon_\Theta(z_t,t)-\epsilon\|^2\big],\qquad \lambda_t=\log\tfrac{a_t^2}{b_t^2}$$
$\lambda_t$ is the signal-to-noise ratio (log), $\epsilon_\Theta$ the noise prediction implied by $v_\Theta$. $w_t=-\tfrac12\lambda_t'b_t^2$ gives $\mathcal L_{CFM}$; changing the weight changes the loss landscape but not the optimum, so all diffusion variants (eps, v, EDM, cosine, LDM-linear) can be compared in one form.

**(c) Rectified flow and timestep density (Eqs. 13, 18).**
$$z_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon,\quad w_t^{RF}=\tfrac{t}{1-t};\qquad w_t^{\pi}=\tfrac{t}{1-t}\pi(t)$$
Sampling $t$ from a density $\pi(t)$ instead of uniform is equivalent to this reweighting. (The text extraction of Eq. 18 is garbled; the form shown is reconstructed from the printed Eq. 13 weight and the statement that non-uniform $\pi(t)$ equals a weighted loss.) Rationale (Sec. 3.1): at $t=0$ and $t=1$ the optimal prediction is just a mean of $p_1$ or $p_0$, so the task is hardest in the middle.

**(d) Logit-normal sampler (Eq. 19).**
$$\pi_{ln}(t;m,s)=\frac{1}{s\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{t(1-t)}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\mathrm{logit}(t)-m)^2}{2s^2}\Big)$$
$m$ shifts mass toward data (negative) or noise (positive), $s$ sets the width; implemented by passing $u\sim\mathcal N(m,s)$ through the logistic function. Also introduced: a heavy-tailed "mode" sampler (Eq. 20) and "CosMap" (Eqs. 21-22) matching the cosine schedule's log-SNR.

**(e) Resolution-dependent timestep shift (Eqs. 23-25).**
$$t_m=\frac{\sqrt{m/n}\,t_n}{1+(\sqrt{m/n}-1)t_n},\qquad \lambda_{t_m}=\lambda_{t_n}-2\log\sqrt{m/n}$$
$n,m$ are the pixel counts of a base and a target resolution. Derived from the uncertainty $\sigma(t,n)=\frac{t}{1-t}\sqrt{1/n}$ of recovering a constant image from $n$ noisy pixels, so a higher resolution needs a noisier schedule. The paper's human study favoured shifts above 1.5 and uses $\alpha=\sqrt{m/n}=3.0$ at 1024x1024.

## 4. Method / architecture / algorithm
1. Work in the latent space of a pretrained autoencoder (downsampling 8); 16 latent channels chosen over 4 or 8 (Sec. 5.2.1, Table 3: FID 2.41 / 1.56 / 1.06 for 4 / 8 / 16 channels).
2. Text: frozen CLIP-L/14, OpenCLIP bigG/14 and T5-XXL; pooled CLIP vectors give a conditioning vector (via modulation), and CLIP plus T5 token sequences give the context (154x4096; App. B.2). Each encoder is dropped with probability 46.3-46.4% in training.
3. MM-DiT: 2x2 latent patches plus text tokens are embedded, then concatenated; each modality has its own weights (QKV, MLP, modulation) but attention runs jointly over the concatenated sequence (Fig. 2). Size is set by depth $d$: hidden size $64d$, $d$ heads.
4. Train with $\mathcal L_{CFM}$ and logit-normal $\pi_{ln}(t;0,1)$. Captions are a 50/50 mix of original and CogVLM synthetic captions (Sec. 5.2.2).
5. Add RMSNorm on Q and K to stop attention-logit growth and enable bf16-mixed training at high resolution (Sec. 5.3.2).
6. Pretrain at 256^2 (500k steps, batch 4096 for the scaling study), then finetune on mixed aspect ratios with bucketed sampling and shifted timesteps, then DPO alignment with LoRA of rank 128 (App. C). Sampling uses Euler discretization (App. B.3).

## 5. Achievements
- Large study of 61 formulations on ImageNet and CC12M (Sec. 5.1): non-dominated rank, Table 1: rf/lognorm(0.00, 1.00) has the best overall mean rank (1.54), versus eps/linear 2.88, plain rf 5.67, edm(-1.20, 1.20) 15.58. Only rectified flow variants with modified timestep sampling beat LDM-Linear. Rectified flows degrade less at few sampling steps (Fig. 3).
- MM-DiT beats DiT, CrossDiT and UViT on validation loss, CLIP score and FID (Fig. 4); a third weight set gives only a small gain.
- Synthetic captions: GenEval overall 43.27 (original) to 49.78 (50/50 mix) (Table 4).
- GenEval (Table 5): depth=38 at 512^2 scores 0.68 (DALL-E 3 0.67, SDXL 0.55), 0.71 with DPO, and 0.74 at 1024^2 with DPO.
- Scaling: validation loss decreases smoothly with model size and steps for image and video, with no saturation, and correlates strongly with GenEval, T2I-CompBench and human preference (Sec. 5.3.3, Fig. 8). The largest model has 8B parameters and 5x10^22 training FLOPs (Conclusion). Larger models need fewer sampling steps: relative CLIP score drop at 5 vs 50 steps is 4.30% (depth 15), 3.59% (30), 2.71% (38) (Table 6).
- Human preference on Parti-prompts: the 8B model beats the compared open and closed models in visual aesthetics, prompt following and typography (Fig. 7; wins are reported as a figure, exact percentages not in the extracted text). Dropping T5 at inference: 50% aesthetics, 46% prompt following, 38% typography win rate (Sec. 5.3.3).

## 6. Limitations
**Stated by the authors:** QK-normalization "is not a universal recipe and may need to be adapted" (Sec. 5.3.2). Pre-encoding precludes per-epoch random augmentation (App. E.1). The depth=38 run needed a learning-rate adjustment at 3x10^5 steps to avoid divergence (Fig. 8 caption). Dropping T5 harms complex-detail and long-text prompts (Fig. 9). Broader-impact section declares no specific societal issues to highlight. No dedicated limitations section.
**Seen in hindsight (hindsight):** The study confounds several changes at once (timestep sampler, autoencoder, captions, architecture, data filtering), and the final comparison to closed models relies on a proprietary-style data pipeline that cannot be reproduced from the paper. Evaluation leans on GenEval, CLIP-based metrics and a human study run by the authors. High cost: T5-XXL alone adds large memory and, per Table 7, about 98% to the 2B model's step time if not precomputed.
