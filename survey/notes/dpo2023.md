# Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model
**Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning, Finn (Stanford / CZ Biohub), NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2305.18290v3)** · **Key:** `dpo2023` · **OpenAlex citations:** 11144 · **Era:** tf
**Read status:** full text for Sections 1-7 and the hyperparameter appendix; derivation appendices (A.1-A.6) located and cross-checked against the main-text sketch but not read line by line.

## 1. Problem and motivation
RLHF (as in `instructgpt2022`) aligns a language model in three phases: SFT, fit a reward model on human preference pairs, then optimise the policy with RL (usually PPO) against that reward. The paper says this is "complex and often unstable", involves training multiple LMs, and requires sampling from the policy inside the training loop at significant compute cost (Abstract, Sec. 1). The goal is to get the same behaviour from a simple supervised-style loss.

## 2. Core idea in one paragraph
The standard RLHF objective (maximise reward minus a KL penalty to a reference model) has a known closed-form optimal policy: the reference policy re-weighted by the exponentiated reward. Turn that relationship around: the reward can be written as a log-ratio between the optimal policy and the reference policy (plus a term depending only on the prompt). Plug this into the Bradley-Terry preference model and the prompt-only term cancels, so human preferences can be expressed directly in terms of the policy. Then fit the policy by maximum likelihood on the preference pairs, with a plain binary cross-entropy loss. The language model itself plays the role of the reward model ("secretly a reward model"), and no sampling or RL loop is needed. Analogy: instead of training a judge and then a student against the judge, you train the student directly on "A beat B" records, with the judge hidden inside the student's own probabilities.

## 3. The mathematics
**Eq. (3), the standard KL-constrained RLHF objective:**
$$\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim D,\,y\sim\pi_\theta(y|x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}\big[\pi_\theta(y|x)\,\|\,\pi_{ref}(y|x)\big]$$
| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\pi_\theta$ | policy being trained |
| $\pi_{ref}$ | reference policy (the SFT model) |
| $r_\phi(x,y)$ | learned reward model |
| $\beta$ | strength of the KL constraint |

**Eq. (4) and (5), optimal policy and reward inversion:**
$$\pi_r(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{ref}(y|x)\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big),\qquad r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$$
$Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{ref}(y|x)\exp(\tfrac1\beta r(x,y))$ is the partition function (intractable to estimate, derivation in App. A.1). Taking the log of (4) and rearranging gives (5).

**Eq. (1), Bradley-Terry preference model**, $p^*(y_1\succ y_2|x)=\sigma(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2))$. Because it depends only on the reward difference, $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels when (5) is substituted, giving Eq. (6): $p^*(y_1\succ y_2|x)=\big[1+\exp\big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_2|x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_1|x)}\big)\big]^{-1}$.

**Eq. (7), the DPO loss:**
$$\mathcal{L}_{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{ref})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim D}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)}\Big)\Big]$$
| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $x$ | prompt |
| $y_w, y_l$ | preferred and dispreferred completions |
| $D$ | static offline dataset of preference pairs |
| $\sigma$ | logistic sigmoid |

Meaning: it is the reward-model loss of Eq. (2) with the reward replaced by the implicit reward $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}$.

**Gradient (Sec. 4):**
$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{DPO}=-\beta\,\mathbb{E}\big[\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\,(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w|x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l|x))\big]$$
Raises the likelihood of $y_w$, lowers that of $y_l$, weighted by how wrongly the implicit reward currently orders the pair. The authors report that dropping this weight makes the model degenerate (Appendix Table 3 per text).

**Theory (Sec. 5.1):** Lemma 1/2: rewards differing by a function $f(x)$ induce the same preferences and the same optimal policy (equivalence classes). Theorem 1: under mild assumptions every reward class consistent with Plackett-Luce / Bradley-Terry can be written as $\beta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}$, so the reparameterisation loses no generality. Sec. 5.2 argues PPO's normaliser $\log\sum_y\pi_{ref}\exp(r/\beta)$ is a soft value function whose absence causes high-variance gradients, while DPO needs no baseline.

## 4. Method / algorithm
1. Collect (or reuse) a preference dataset of $(x,y_w,y_l)$; ideally sampled from $\pi_{ref}$. Set $\pi_{ref}=\pi^{SFT}$; if no SFT model exists, fine-tune on the preferred completions to get one (Sec. 4).
2. Minimise Eq. (7) with ordinary gradient descent; no sampling during training.
3. Defaults (App. B): $\beta=0.1$, batch size 64, RMSprop, learning rate 1e-6 with linear warmup over 150 steps; for TL;DR summarization $\beta=0.5$.

## 5. Achievements
- Controlled IMDb sentiment (GPT-2-large SFT): DPO gives the best reward-vs-KL frontier, strictly dominating PPO, even PPO given the ground-truth reward (PPO-GT); 22 runs in the sweep (Sec. 6.1, Fig. 2 left).
- TL;DR summarization (GPT-J SFT, GPT-4 judge vs reference summaries): DPO about 61% win rate at temperature 0.0 vs PPO about 57% at its best temperature (Sec. 6.2, Fig. 2 right); DPO is more robust to sampling temperature. Human study: DPO samples (temp 0.25) preferred over PPO (temp 0) 58% of the time (Sec. 6.2).
- Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogue (Pythia-2.8B): the only computationally efficient method that improves on the chosen completions; matches or exceeds Best-of-128 (Sec. 6.2, Fig. 3). A public PPO-HH Pythia-6B checkpoint could not be made to beat the base Pythia-2.8B (Sec. 6.2).
- Out-of-distribution (CNN/DailyMail): win rate 0.36 (temp 0) and 0.31 (temp 0.25) for DPO vs 0.26 and 0.23 for PPO (Table 1).
- GPT-4 as judge validated: GPT-4-human agreement similar to human-human (Table 2; e.g. 67-70% vs 65% for DPO-vs-PPO).

## 6. Limitations
**Stated by the authors (Sec. 7):** out-of-distribution generalisation vs explicit-reward methods needs fuller study; how reward over-optimisation manifests in DPO is unknown (slight decrease in Fig. 3 right may be an instance); only models up to 6B parameters evaluated; GPT-4 win rates depend on the prompt (GPT-4 (S) preferred longer summaries); DPO's $\beta$ was not meaningfully tuned.
**Seen in hindsight (hindsight):** offline pairs come from a different policy than the one being trained, so DPO can suffer distribution shift; later work reported that DPO can lower the likelihood of both chosen and rejected responses and tends to over-weight length; it needs a reference model in memory and has no online exploration, so it does not discover behaviours beyond the data.

## 7. Place in the lineage
- **Builds on:** `instructgpt2022` (RLHF pipeline), `ppo2017` (the optimiser it replaces), Bradley-Terry / Plackett-Luce preference models, Ziegler et al. 2019 and Stiennon et al. 2020 (RLHF), KL-regularised RL / control-as-inference.
- **Led to:** a large family of direct-preference methods and widely used open-model alignment recipes (hindsight); in the set, a contrast with `r1_2025`, which instead uses online RL with rule-based rewards.
- **Single idea added:** a change of variables that turns the KL-regularised RLHF problem into a binary classification loss on log-probability ratios, removing the reward model and the RL loop.
