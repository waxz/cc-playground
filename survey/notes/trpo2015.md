# Trust Region Policy Optimization
**Schulman, Levine, Moritz, Jordan, Abbeel (UC Berkeley), ICML 2015 (arXiv:1502.05477, v5 text)** · **Key:** `trpo2015` · **OpenAlex citations:** 7620 · **Era:** rl
**Read status:** full text for main body (two-column layout was interleaved by extraction but readable) and Appendix C (efficient solution); Appendices A-B (proofs) and D-E (architectures, parameters) not read in detail.

## 1. Problem and motivation
Policy optimisation methods fell into policy iteration, policy gradient and derivative-free methods (CEM, CMA). Derivative-free methods were hard to beat for large nonlinear policies, even though gradient-based methods have better sample complexity guarantees (Sec. 1). Standard policy gradient needs a step size that is hard to choose: too large and performance collapses, too small and learning is slow. The goal is a gradient-based method for large neural-network policies with a principled, near-monotonic improvement guarantee and little tuning.

## 2. Core idea in one paragraph
Instead of taking a fixed-size step in parameter space, take the largest step that keeps the new policy close to the old one, measured by KL divergence in action distributions. Theory shows that maximising a local surrogate of the return, minus a penalty on the KL divergence, guarantees the true return cannot drop. In practice the penalty is replaced by a hard constraint (a "trust region") on average KL, solved approximately with a natural-gradient direction via conjugate gradient and a backtracking line search. It is like walking on fog-covered ground: you trust your local map only within a limited radius, and you step no farther than that radius.

## 3. The mathematics
**Return identity (Eq. 1-2, from Kakade and Langford):**
$$\eta(\tilde\pi)=\eta(\pi)+\sum_s\rho_{\tilde\pi}(s)\sum_a\tilde\pi(a\mid s)A_\pi(s,a)$$
$\eta(\pi)=\mathbb{E}[\sum_t\gamma^tr(s_t)]$ expected discounted return; $A_\pi=Q_\pi-V_\pi$ advantage; $\rho_\pi(s)=\sum_t\gamma^tP(s_t=s)$ unnormalised discounted visitation. The new policy's return equals the old return plus its expected advantage under the old policy's value function.

**Local surrogate (Eq. 3-4):**
$$L_\pi(\tilde\pi)=\eta(\pi)+\sum_s\rho_\pi(s)\sum_a\tilde\pi(a\mid s)A_\pi(s,a)$$
Uses the old visitation $\rho_\pi$ instead of $\rho_{\tilde\pi}$. It matches $\eta$ to first order: $L_{\pi_{\theta_0}}(\pi_{\theta_0})=\eta(\pi_{\theta_0})$ and gradients agree at $\theta_0$, but this gives no guidance on step size.

**Improvement bound (Theorem 1, Eq. 8-9):** with $\alpha=D_{TV}^{\max}(\pi_{old},\pi_{new})$ and $\epsilon=\max_{s,a}|A_\pi(s,a)|$,
$$\eta(\pi_{new})\ge L_{\pi_{old}}(\pi_{new})-\frac{4\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}\alpha^2,$$
and using $D_{TV}^2\le D_{KL}$:
$$\eta(\tilde\pi)\ge L_\pi(\tilde\pi)-C\,D_{KL}^{\max}(\pi,\tilde\pi),\quad C=\frac{4\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}.$$
(The paper's Eq. 9 writes $C=4\gamma/(1-\gamma)^2$ with $\epsilon$ folded into the constant; the version above follows Theorem 1.) Maximising the right-hand side at each iteration is a minorisation-maximisation scheme (Algorithm 1), so $\eta(\pi_0)\le\eta(\pi_1)\le\cdots$.

**Practical constrained problem (Eq. 11-12):**
$$\max_\theta L_{\theta_{old}}(\theta)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \overline{D}_{KL}^{\rho_{\theta_{old}}}(\theta_{old},\theta)\le\delta$$
where $\overline D_{KL}=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho}[D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_1}(\cdot|s)\,\|\,\pi_{\theta_2}(\cdot|s))]$ is the average (not max) KL, and $\delta$ the trust-region size ($\delta=0.01$ in all experiments). The theoretical penalty coefficient would give tiny steps; a hard constraint allows larger robust steps; the max over states is replaced by an average because it is intractable.

**Sample-based form (Eq. 14):**
$$\max_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho_{\theta_{old}},a\sim q}\Big[\frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{q(a|s)}Q_{\theta_{old}}(s,a)\Big]\ \text{s.t.}\ \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho_{\theta_{old}}}[D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\|\pi_\theta)]\le\delta$$
Importance sampling with sampling distribution $q$; $Q$ replaces $A$ (a constant shift).

**Step computation (Appendix C):** search direction $s\approx A^{-1}g$ where $g$ is the objective gradient and $A_{ij}=\partial_{\theta_i}\partial_{\theta_j}D_{KL}$ is the Fisher information matrix (quadratic approximation $D_{KL}\approx\tfrac12(\theta-\theta_{old})^\top A(\theta-\theta_{old})$); maximal step $\beta=\sqrt{2\delta/s^\top As}$; then shrink $\beta$ exponentially (line search) until the surrogate improves and the KL constraint holds.

## 4. Method / algorithm
1. Collect state-action pairs with Monte Carlo Q estimates by either single path (ordinary trajectories) or vine (branch rollouts from a rollout set of states, several actions per state, common random numbers; needs resettable simulator) (Sec. 5).
2. Build the sample estimate of the objective and the constraint.
3. Solve approximately: conjugate gradient using Fisher-vector products (no explicit matrix; $k=10$ CG iterations found effective), then line search. The authors note that without the line search the algorithm occasionally takes large steps that degrade performance catastrophically (App. C).
4. Special cases: linearising $L$ and quadratically approximating the KL gives natural policy gradient (Eq. 17); an $\ell_2$ constraint gives vanilla policy gradient (Eq. 18); unconstrained maximisation of $L$ gives policy iteration (Sec. 7).

## 5. Achievements
- Locomotion (MuJoCo; swimmer, hopper, walker; Fig. 4, averaged over five runs): single path and vine TRPO solved all problems with general-purpose neural policies and simple rewards; natural gradient with a fixed penalty failed to produce forward-moving hopping and walking; CEM, CMA performed poorly on larger problems; the max-KL variant (tractable on cartpole only) learned somewhat slower than average-KL (Sec. 8.1).
- Atari (seven games, raw pixels, 33,500-parameter convnet policy, Table 1, one run each): TRPO single path scored B. Rider 1425.2, Breakout 10.8, Enduro 534.6, Pong 20.9, Q*bert 1973.5, Seaquest 1908.6, S. Invaders 568.4; vine: 859.5, 34.2, 430.8, 20.9, 7732.5, 788.4, 450.2. DQN (Mnih 2013) got 4092/168/470/20/1952/1705/581; UCC-I got 5702/380/741/21/20025/2995/692. The authors say it only outperformed prior methods on some games but consistently achieved reasonable scores. 500 iterations took about 30 hours on a 16-core computer.
- Showed one generic algorithm covers both continuous control and image-based games.

## 6. Limitations
**Stated by the authors:** the theory ignores advantage estimation error, and the practical algorithm deviates from the theory (penalty to constraint, max KL to average KL) (Sec. 6); the vine method needs resettable simulation so is simulator-only and costs many more simulator calls (Sec. 5.2); Atari results come from single runs with no error statistics due to time constraints (Table 1 caption); results vary substantially run to run; only reasonable, not state-of-the-art, scores on Atari; sample complexity could be reduced with model learning (Sec. 9).
**Seen in hindsight (hindsight):** per-update cost of conjugate gradient and Fisher-vector products and on-policy data make it sample-hungry (cf. `ddpg2015` comparison: TRPO "appears significantly less data efficient"); awkward to combine with parameter sharing, noise-injecting architectures, and large batches; superseded in simplicity by `ppo2017`, which uses a clipped surrogate instead of a constrained second-order step.

## 7. Place in the lineage
- **Builds on:** conservative policy iteration (Kakade and Langford 2002), natural policy gradient (Kakade 2002), REPS and relative-entropy methods, `dqn2013` as Atari benchmark.
- **Led to (hindsight):** `ppo2017` (first-order approximation of the trust-region idea), GAE, and use of KL-regularised updates in RLHF (`instructgpt2022`, and `dpo2023` by derivation from the KL-regularised objective); also compared against by `ddpg2015` and `a3c2016`.
- **One idea:** bound the policy change by a KL trust region to obtain stable, near-monotonic improvement for large neural policies.
