# Generative Adversarial Nets
**Authors, venue, year:** Ian J. Goodfellow, Jean Pouget-Abadie, Mehdi Mirza, Bing Xu, David Warde-Farley, Sherjil Ozair, Aaron Courville, Yoshua Bengio (Univ. de Montreal); arXiv:1406.2661v1, June 2014 (venue not printed; NIPS 2014 from background knowledge) · **Key:** `gan2014` · **OpenAlex citations:** 12148 · **Era:** dl
**Read status:** full text

## 1. Problem and motivation
Deep discriminative models had succeeded via backprop, dropout and piecewise linear units, but deep generative models lagged because maximum likelihood needs intractable probabilistic computations (partition functions, MCMC mixing, approximate inference) and because piecewise linear units are hard to exploit in a generative setting (Sec. 1, 2). The paper proposes a generative estimation procedure that avoids these.

## 2. Core idea in one paragraph
Train two networks against each other. A generator turns random noise into samples; a discriminator tries to tell real data from generated samples. The generator is rewarded for fooling the discriminator. The paper's analogy: counterfeiters versus police. If both have enough capacity, the only stable outcome is a generator whose samples are distributed exactly like the data and a discriminator that can do no better than guessing. Everything is trained by backprop; sampling needs only a forward pass, with no Markov chain or inference network.

## 3. The mathematics
**Minimax game (Eq. 1):**
$$\min_G\max_D V(D,G)=\mathbb E_{x\sim p_{data}(x)}[\log D(x)]+\mathbb E_{z\sim p_z(z)}[\log(1-D(G(z)))]$$
| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $G(z;\theta_g)$ | generator MLP mapping noise $z$ to data space; induces distribution $p_g$ |
| $D(x;\theta_d)$ | discriminator MLP; probability that $x$ came from data |
| $p_{data},p_z$ | data distribution, noise prior |

Meaning: $D$ maximises log-likelihood of correct labels; $G$ minimises the discriminator's success.

**Optimal discriminator (Prop. 1, Eq. 2):** $D^*_G(x)=\dfrac{p_{data}(x)}{p_{data}(x)+p_g(x)}$. Proof: pointwise, $a\log y+b\log(1-y)$ is maximised on $[0,1]$ at $a/(a+b)$.

**Virtual criterion and Jensen-Shannon (Eq. 4-6, Thm. 1):**
$$C(G)=\max_D V(G,D)=-\log4+KL\!\Big(p_{data}\Big\|\tfrac{p_{data}+p_g}2\Big)+KL\!\Big(p_g\Big\|\tfrac{p_{data}+p_g}2\Big)=-\log4+2\,JSD(p_{data}\|p_g)$$
Meaning: the generator, against a perfect discriminator, minimises JSD between data and model distribution; the global minimum $-\log4$ is attained iff $p_g=p_{data}$, where $D^*=\tfrac12$.

**Convergence (Prop. 2):** $V$ is convex in $p_g$; if $D$ reaches its optimum each step and $p_g$ is updated to improve $C$, then $p_g\to p_{data}$ (non-parametric setting, infinite capacity).

**Non-saturating trick (Sec. 3):** early on, $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ saturates; train $G$ to maximise $\log D(G(z))$ instead, same fixed point but stronger gradients.

## 4. Method / architecture / algorithm
1. Algorithm 1: per iteration, k steps of: sample $m$ noise and $m$ data samples, ascend $\nabla_{\theta_d}\frac1m\sum[\log D(x^{(i)})+\log(1-D(G(z^{(i)})))]$; then one generator step descending $\nabla_{\theta_g}\frac1m\sum\log(1-D(G(z^{(i)})))$. Experiments use k = 1 and momentum.
2. Generator: rectifier plus sigmoid activations, noise only at the bottom layer; discriminator: maxout units with dropout (Sec. 5).
3. Evaluation via Gaussian Parzen-window log-likelihood on generated samples, $\sigma$ cross-validated.
4. Datasets: MNIST, Toronto Face Database (TFD), CIFAR-10.

## 5. Achievements
- Table 1 (Parzen log-likelihood): MNIST 225 ± 2 for adversarial nets vs DBN 138 ± 2, Stacked CAE 121 ± 1.6, Deep GSN 214 ± 1.1; TFD 2057 ± 26 vs DBN 1909 ± 66, Stacked CAE 2110 ± 50, Deep GSN 1890 ± 29 (Stacked CAE is higher on TFD).
- Figs. 2-3: samples on MNIST, TFD, CIFAR-10 (fully connected and convolutional), with nearest training neighbours shown and interpolations in $z$; stated as "fair random draws, not cherry-picked". The authors claim samples are "at least competitive" but make no claim of being better.
- Table 2 compares against directed, undirected and autoencoder-based generative models on training, inference, sampling and model design.

## 6. Limitations
**Stated by the authors:** no explicit representation of $p_g(x)$; $D$ must be synchronised with $G$, else $G$ collapses many $z$ to the same $x$ ("Helvetica scenario") (Sec. 6); the Parzen estimator has high variance and fails in high dimensions (Sec. 5); theory is non-parametric and MLPs bring multiple critical points without guarantees (Sec. 4.2).
**Seen in hindsight (hindsight):** training instability and mode collapse became central problems; JSD gives vanishing gradients with disjoint supports (motivating Wasserstein GANs); sample-quality evaluation needed later metrics (Inception Score, FID); diffusion models later superseded GANs in image synthesis.

## 7. Place in the lineage
- **Builds on:** `vae2013` (cited as related), dropout (`dropout2014`, Hinton et al. 2012 as cited), maxout, GSNs, noise-contrastive estimation.
- **Led to:** conditional/progressive/StyleGAN-style image generators (hindsight); adversarial ideas in `ddpm2020`'s rivals (hindsight); the paper itself lists conditional generation, inference nets and semi-supervised use as extensions (Sec. 7).
- **Single idea added:** train a generative model by pitting it against a learned discriminator in a minimax game, avoiding likelihoods, MCMC and inference.

## 8. Verification notes
Algorithm 1 in the text writes the generator step as descending $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ (the non-saturating alternative is described only in prose). The paper's own Prop. 2 proof is brief; not independently checked. NIPS venue is from background knowledge.
