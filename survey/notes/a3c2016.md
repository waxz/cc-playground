# Asynchronous Methods for Deep Reinforcement Learning
**Mnih, Badia, Mirza, Graves, Harley, Lillicrap, Silver, Kavukcuoglu (Google DeepMind; Mirza also MILA), ICML 2016 (arXiv:1602.01783v2)** · **Key:** `a3c2016` · **OpenAlex citations:** 10950 · **Era:** rl
**Read status:** full text for the main body (two-column layout interleaved but readable) and the supplementary setup/optimisation sections; full per-game score tables and supplementary algorithm boxes S2/S3 not reproduced in the extraction I read.

## 1. Problem and motivation
Online RL updates with deep networks are strongly correlated and non-stationary, so earlier systems stabilised them with experience replay (Sec. 1). Replay costs memory and computation per interaction and restricts methods to off-policy algorithms. Prior deep RL also leaned on GPUs or massive distributed systems (Gorila used 100 actor-learners and 30 parameter servers, 130 machines, Sec. 2).

## 2. Core idea in one paragraph
Run many actor-learner threads in parallel on one multi-core CPU, each with its own copy of the environment, all updating one shared set of network parameters without locks (Hogwild-style). Because different threads are in different parts of the environment, and may use different exploration policies, the combined stream of updates is far less correlated, so the parallelism plays the stabilising role of the replay buffer. This works with on-policy methods too. The flagship variant, asynchronous advantage actor-critic (A3C), updates a policy and a value function from short n-step rollouts. It is like many people each practising a different part of a game and all contributing notes to one shared notebook.

## 3. The mathematics
**One-step Q-learning loss (Sec. 3):**
$$L_i(\theta_i)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta_{i-1})-Q(s,a;\theta_i)\big)^2\Big]$$
$s'$ next state, $\gamma$ discount, $\theta_{i-1}$ older parameters (the target network $\theta^-$ in Algorithm 1). A reward only directly updates the preceding state-action pair, so reward propagation is slow.

**n-step return (Sec. 3):** $r_t+\gamma r_{t+1}+\dots+\gamma^{n-1}r_{t+n-1}+\max_a\gamma^nQ(s_{t+n},a)$, which lets one reward directly affect $n$ preceding pairs.

**Policy gradient with baseline (Sec. 3):** REINFORCE direction $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a_t|s_t;\theta)(R_t-b_t(s_t))$, with $b_t\approx V^\pi(s_t)$. $R_t-b_t$ estimates the advantage $A(a_t,s_t)=Q(a_t,s_t)-V(s_t)$; the baseline cuts variance without adding bias.

**A3C update (Sec. 4):**
$$\nabla_{\theta'}\log\pi(a_t|s_t;\theta')\,A(s_t,a_t;\theta,\theta_v)+\beta\nabla_{\theta'}H(\pi(s_t;\theta')),\quad A=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\gamma^ir_{t+i}+\gamma^kV(s_{t+k};\theta_v)-V(s_t;\theta_v)$$
$\theta$ policy parameters, $\theta_v$ value parameters (mostly shared in practice: a convnet with a softmax policy head and a linear value head), $k\le t_{max}$ (variable per state), $H$ policy entropy, $\beta$ its weight. The entropy term discourages premature convergence to deterministic policies. The advantage is the $k$-step bootstrapped return minus the value baseline.

**Shared RMSProp (Eq. 1):** $g=\alpha g+(1-\alpha)\Delta\theta^2$, $\;\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\Delta\theta/\sqrt{g+\epsilon}$, element-wise, with $\Delta\theta$ the gradient estimate, $\alpha$ the decay, $\eta$ the learning rate; the statistics $g$ are shared across threads, which was found considerably more robust than unshared RMSProp or momentum SGD.

## 4. Method / algorithm
1. Each of 16 threads copies the global parameters, acts for up to $t_{max}=5$ steps (or to terminal) with its own exploration, then computes n-step returns for every state in the segment using the longest available return.
2. Accumulate gradients over the segment (like a minibatch) and apply them asynchronously and lock-free to the shared parameters.
3. Value-based variants (one-step Sarsa, one-step Q, n-step Q) use a shared target network updated every 40000 frames; each thread samples its $\epsilon$ from a distribution (three final values 0.1, 0.01, 0.5 with probabilities 0.4, 0.3, 0.3, annealed over four million frames).
4. A3C: entropy weight $\beta=0.01$; $\gamma=0.99$; RMSProp decay $0.99$; network follows Mnih et al. 2013 (conv 16 8x8 stride 4, conv 32 4x4 stride 2, FC 256), optionally plus 256 LSTM cells (supplementary Sec. 8). Action repeat 4.

## 5. Achievements
- Fig. 1: all four asynchronous methods train Atari controllers on 16 CPU cores, faster than DQN on one K40 GPU on five games; A3C beats the three value-based variants.
- Table 1 (57 Atari games, human starts, mean / median human-normalised): DQN 121.9% / 47.5% (8 days GPU); Gorila 215.2% / 71.3%; Dueling Double DQN 343.8% / 117.1%; Prioritized DQN 463.6% / 127.6%; A3C FF 1 day CPU 344.1% / 68.2%, 4 days 496.8% / 116.6%; A3C LSTM 4 days 623.0% / 112.6%. A3C improves the state-of-the-art mean in half the training time of the others with no GPU.
- Table 2: speed-up with 16 threads, averaged over seven games: 1-step Q 24.1x, 1-step Sarsa 22.1x, n-step Q 17.2x, A3C 12.5x; one-step methods show superlinear speed-ups that the authors attribute to reduced bias (Sec. 5.5).
- Fig. 2: with 50 learning rates and seeds per game, a wide range of learning rates works for all seeds (robust and stable).
- TORCS: A3C reaches roughly 75% to 90% of a human tester's score in about 12 hours (Sec. 5.2). MuJoCo continuous control: good solutions in under 24 hours, typically a few hours (Sec. 5.3). Labyrinth (random 3D mazes, A3C LSTM from 84x84 RGB): average score around 50 (Sec. 5.4).

## 6. Limitations
**Stated by the authors:** removing replay does not mean replay is useless; adding it could greatly improve data efficiency, notably where environment interaction is expensive (Sec. 6); Double DQN and dueling improvements were not incorporated (though the authors say they could be); hyperparameters were tuned on six games and then fixed for all 57.
**Seen in hindsight (hindsight):** A3C's median human-normalised score (116.6% at 4 days) is below Prioritized DQN (127.6%, Table 1) even though its mean is higher; asynchronous lock-free updates are non-deterministic and hard to reproduce; the synchronous version (A2C) with batched GPU inference was later found as good and simpler; on-policy methods are less sample-efficient than replay-based ones; stale-gradient effects limit scaling; later superseded by `ppo2017`-style and distributed learner designs (IMPALA).

## 7. Place in the lineage
- **Builds on:** `dqn2013` (target networks, Atari setup), Gorila, Hogwild!, REINFORCE and actor-critic (Williams 1992; Sutton and Barto), n-step returns; related prior policy optimisation `trpo2015`.
- **Led to (hindsight):** A2C, `ppo2017` (typically run with parallel actors), IMPALA/UNREAL, and parallel-actor training as the default RL engineering pattern.
- **One idea:** parallel, differently exploring actor-learners decorrelate data, making replay-free, on-policy deep RL (A3C) stable on a CPU.
