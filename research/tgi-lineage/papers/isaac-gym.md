## 1. Problem and position in the lineage

Added at depth 3 because it is the substrate of the massively parallel paradigm: [legged_gym](legged-gym.md), [Radosavovic et al.](radosavovic-humanoid.md), [Extreme Parkour](extreme-parkour.md), [humanoid parkour](humanoid-parkour.md), [BeamDojo](beamdojo.md), [Zhang et al.](risky-terrains.md), [ANYmal parkour](anymal-parkour.md), [PHC](phc.md), [MaskedMimic](maskedmimic.md) and [ASAP](asap.md) all train in it, and its successor Isaac Lab hosts [Watch Your Step](watch-your-step.md) and [TGI](tgi.md). The problem it addresses is throughput. CPU simulators (MuJoCo, PyBullet, DART, Drake) run roughly one environment per core, so large RL runs required clusters: the paper cites OpenAI's in-hand cube reorientation, trained on 384 machines with 6144 CPU cores plus 8 V100 GPUs for 30 hours, and the Rubik's-cube follow-up on almost 30 000 cores. An earlier GPU simulator by the same group still copied physics state to the CPU every step to compute observations and rewards, and copied actions back. Isaac Gym removes that round trip.

## 2. Method

**End-to-end GPU pipeline.** Two changes were made to PhysX: GPU simulations can run without fetching results to the CPU after every step, and a direct GPU API reads state, writes state changes and applies controls in device buffers. On top of this, the **Tensor API** exposes simulator buffers as flat global tensors that are wrapped as PyTorch tensors without copying (CUDA interoperability). The state tensors are actor root states (position, quaternion, linear and angular velocity, 13 values per actor), DOF states (position and velocity), rigid-body states, DOF forces, force-sensor wrenches, net contact forces per body, and Jacobian and generalised mass matrices for homogeneous groups of articulated actors. Root and DOF state tensors are writable and are used to reset any subset of environments through an index buffer. Control tensors carry DOF actuation forces, PD position and velocity targets (with drive stiffness and damping configured separately) and rigid-body forces and torques. Observation and reward computations are therefore vectorised GPU kernels over an environment dimension, and policy inference and PPO updates read the same memory.

**Scene structure.** Assets are loaded from URDF or MJCF; multi-body actors are PhysX reduced-coordinate articulations. Thousands of environment copies are packed into one scene without mutual interaction, the opposite of the CPU practice of one scene per thread, because GPUs need many thousands of objects to fill their streaming multiprocessors. Most joint and body properties can be changed during simulation, which supports per-environment domain randomisation ([domain randomisation](../math-cookbook.md#m-domain-rand)). Fixed tendons are supported for the Shadow Hand.

**Solver.** Contacts and joints are solved by the Temporal Gauss–Seidel (TGS) solver. It exploits the observation that sub-stepping with one Gauss–Seidel iteration per sub-step converges faster than fewer, larger steps with more iterations, and folds this into the iteration: after each iteration the body velocities, scaled by $dt/N$, are accumulated into a delta buffer that is projected onto the constraint Jacobians and added to the constraint bias terms. The effect approximates sub-stepping at almost the per-iteration cost of plain Gauss–Seidel. Users tune time step, position and velocity iteration counts, bias limits, restitution, friction coefficients and offsets, contact offsets, joint friction and armature ([rigid contact](../math-cookbook.md#m-rigid-contact), [PD control](../math-cookbook.md#m-pd-actuator)).

**Learning setup.** All environments are trained with PPO from rl_games, a GPU-vectorised implementation, with $\gamma = 0.99$ (0.998 for LSTMs), GAE $\lambda = 0.95$, clipping 0.2 and an adaptive learning rate ([PPO](../math-cookbook.md#m-ppo-clip), [GAE](../math-cookbook.md#m-advantage-gae)). The Shadow Hand and TriFinger tasks use an asymmetric actor–critic in which the critic receives privileged simulator state.

## 3. Evidence

All results are on one A100 with a single Intel i7-8700K CPU, averaged over five seeds. Ant reaches "performant locomotion" (reward 3000) in about 20 s, and the time to reward 7000 falls from about 1000 s with 256 environments to 100 s with 8192, at up to about 700 000 environment steps per second; with a fixed experience budget per update, going from 8192 to 16 384 environments brought no gain because the PPO horizon became too short. Humanoid walks after about 4 minutes (reward 5000) at about 200 000 steps per second; beyond 4096 environments the 16-step horizons degraded the gait unless the horizon was doubled. ANYmal velocity tracking on flat ground is learned in under 2 minutes with 4096 environments, and a rough-terrain task with height scans, observation noise, friction randomisation, pushes, an actuator network and an automatic terrain curriculum trains in under 20 minutes on an RTX A6000 and transfers to the real robot (the method later published as [legged_gym](legged-gym.md)). AMP character animation needs about 39 million samples, about 6 minutes, against about 30 hours on 16 CPU cores in PyBullet for the original implementation, a 300× speed-up. The OpenAI-style Shadow Hand with domain randomisation reaches 20 consecutive successes in under 1 hour with a feed-forward policy and 37 with an LSTM in about 6 hours (2.5 hours for the best seed), against 30 and 17 hours for OpenAI's setup; TriFinger cube reposing transfers to the real robot with a mean success rate of 55 %.

## 4. Critical analysis

The speed-ups compare wall-clock time to a reward threshold, measured on tasks chosen by the authors with their own PPO implementation. They characterise throughput, not sample efficiency: PPO in Isaac Gym typically consumes more samples than in CPU setups, and the scaling studies show that, at a fixed batch size, more environments mean shorter rollouts and eventually worse policies. The comparison with OpenAI's Shadow Hand is not like for like (continuous rather than discrete actions, no action delay or backlash randomisation, a different network order, five seeds against one, no sim-to-real for the hand), which the authors list explicitly. The sim-to-real evidence is ANYmal locomotion and TriFinger manipulation, both reported briefly with details deferred to other papers.

PhysX in Isaac Gym is **not differentiable**: gradients of the next state with respect to actions are unavailable, and the iterative TGS contact handling would make them unreliable even if exposed ([contact gradients](../math-cookbook.md#m-contact-gradients)). The paper itself names Brax as an approach "similar" in running end-to-end on accelerators. Isaac Gym therefore made zeroth-order estimators such as PPO cheap without making first-order methods possible, and the subsequent dominance of PPO in legged locomotion is partly an artefact of where compute became cheap. It also cannot represent closed kinematic chains, the workaround discussed by [Radosavovic et al.](radosavovic-humanoid.md).

## 6. Relevance to TGI

TGI depends on this lineage's throughput (8192 G1 environments in Isaac Lab) to generate world-model data and imagination start states at a rate of about 2.5 simulator steps per policy update. Because the industrial GPU simulator offers no usable gradients, TGI fits a world model to its transitions and differentiates that model instead, the same choice as [SuperMimic](supermimic.md) and [RWM](rwm.md); the simulator's net contact forces per body, exposed by the Tensor API family, become TGI's force labels for the contact-structured model ([model learning](../math-cookbook.md#m-model-learning)). Isaac Gym thus explains both why PPO dominated the period and why first-order successors go through a learned model rather than the simulator.
