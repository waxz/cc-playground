# g5 — Learned legged locomotion: PPO in massively parallel simulation, from ANYmal to sparse footholds

This group traces the paradigm that TGI is written against: model-free policy optimisation (TRPO, then [PPO](../papers/ppo.md)) in simulation, transferred zero-shot to legged hardware by a combination of learned actuator models, domain randomisation, privileged teacher–student training, terrain curricula and heightmap perception. Between 2019 and 2025 this recipe moved from blind flat-ground trotting on a quadruped to perceptive parkour and, finally, to humanoids on stepping stones and balance beams. The last step is where the paradigm visibly strains, and the analysis below argues that the strain is a property of the gradient estimator rather than of the robots, the simulators or the network architectures. That argument is the bridge to [g3](g3-analytic-gradients.md) and to [TGI](../papers/tgi.md).

## 1. The problem

A legged controller must map proprioception and, increasingly, exteroception to joint commands at 50 Hz on hardware whose actuators, contacts and terrain are poorly modelled. The classical answer ([g4](g4-classical-control.md)) decomposes the problem into template-model planning, foothold selection and whole-body tracking, each with explicit models and online optimisation. The learned answer collapses the stack into one network optimised against a reward in simulation, and must solve three subsidiary problems: the *reality gap* (simulation is not the robot), *partial observability* (the deployed robot does not see what the simulator knows), and *exploration* (the reward landscape must be navigable by a stochastic policy). The papers in this group are, in large part, a catalogue of devices for each.

## 2. Chronological development

**Closing the actuator gap (2019).** [Hwangbo et al.](../papers/hwangbo-anymal.md) showed that the reality gap for a robot with series-elastic actuators is dominated by the actuator, not the rigid-body dynamics, and closed it with a supervised *actuator network* inside an otherwise analytical simulator ([actuator networks](../math-cookbook.md#m-pd-actuator)). Policies outputting PD position targets, trained with TRPO on about $2.5\times10^8$ transitions in four hours, transferred to ANYmal and beat the existing model-based controller in tracking accuracy and efficiency. Two habits that persist through the lineage appear here: a curriculum on penalty coefficients to escape the stand-still optimum, and long discounts ($\gamma = 0.9988$).

**Privileged information and adaptation (2020–2021).** [Lee et al.](../papers/lee-challenging-terrain.md) separated the problem into a teacher trained by RL with privileged terrain and contact information and a proprioceptive TCN student trained by DAgger to reproduce the teacher's action and latent ([teacher–student](../math-cookbook.md#m-domain-rand), [distillation](../math-cookbook.md#m-distillation)). They also introduced the first adaptive terrain curriculum, a particle filter tracking terrains of traversability in $[0.5, 0.9]$ ([curricula](../math-cookbook.md#m-curriculum)). The blind controller crossed mud, snow and vegetation and ran DARPA SubT missions without falls. [RMA](../papers/rma.md) reformulated adaptation on the cheap Unitree A1: a base policy conditioned on an 8-D extrinsics latent of privileged parameters, and a 1-D CNN that regresses that latent from 0.5 s of history on-policy. RMA used PPO, and from here on PPO is universal. Both papers end with the same admission: a blind robot cannot choose footholds.

**Massive parallelism (2021).** [Isaac Gym](../papers/isaac-gym.md) moved physics, rewards and learning onto one GPU, and [legged_gym](../papers/legged-gym.md) turned this into the canonical recipe: 4096 robots, $B\approx10^5$ samples per update, at least about 25 steps per robot per update for GAE to work, time-out bootstrapping, 108 height samples around the base, a "game-inspired" promotion/demotion curriculum over a tiled terrain grid, and rough-terrain training in under 20 minutes. Its open-source code became the shared substrate; TGI itself runs in Isaac Lab, legged_gym's descendant.

**Robust perception (2022).** [Miki et al.](../papers/miki-wild.md) added exteroception to the teacher–student pipeline, with a GRU belief encoder whose sigmoid gate admits exteroceptive features only when consistent with proprioception, and a three-scope height-noise model (per point, per foot, per episode) simulating drift, missing data and deformable surfaces. Deployed on a [GPU elevation map](../papers/elevation-mapping-gpu.md), the controller completed an Alpine hike in human time. The price of robustness was precision: the authors note that at a cliff or stepping stone "the policy assumes a continuous surface".

**Agility (2023).** [ANYmal parkour](../papers/anymal-parkour.md) built a hierarchy of five PPO skills (walk, jump, climb up/down, crouch) under a position-based, time-budgeted task formulation, a learned 3D reconstruction from point clouds, and a navigation policy with a hybrid categorical–Gaussian PPO actor, reaching 2 m/s over 1 m boxes and gaps. [Extreme Parkour](../papers/extreme-parkour.md) achieved comparable agility on an A1 with one end-to-end depth policy, an inner-product tracking reward towards waypoints, an edge-clearance penalty, and "dual distillation" of both actions and self-chosen headings from scandots to depth.

**Humanoids (2023–2024).** [Radosavovic et al.](../papers/radosavovic-humanoid.md) transferred the recipe to the full-size Digit with a causal transformer student trained by a joint PPO + KL-to-teacher objective, blind but robust outdoors. [Humanoid parkour](../papers/humanoid-parkour.md) brought the scandot-to-depth pipeline to the H1, replacing foot-lifting rewards with fractal terrain noise, but had to add a hand-placed footstep reward on stairs because PPO "does not generate accurate enough action without explicit footstep guidance".

**Sparse footholds (2023–2025).** [Zhang et al.](../papers/risky-terrains.md) produced the first end-to-end RL policy for a quadruped on stepping stones and beams, needing a navigation formulation, a relaxed curriculum, curiosity rewards, four-fold symmetry augmentation ([symmetry](../math-cookbook.md#m-symmetry)) and a generalist policy fine-tuned into terrain-specific specialists that did not transfer to one another. [Attention-based map encoding](../papers/attention-map-encoding.md) obtained one policy across sparse terrains for ANYmal-D and GR-1 with a proprioception-queried multi-head attention over CNN map features ([attention](../math-cookbook.md#m-attention)), trained in two stages over several days. [BeamDojo](../papers/beamdojo.md) put a G1 on stepping stones and beams with an area-based foothold reward, a double critic for dense and sparse rewards, and a first stage on flat ground that shows the policy the sparse terrain without terminating on missteps.

## 4. Shared mathematics

All methods optimise the discounted return of a POMDP ([MDP/POMDP](../math-cookbook.md#m-mdp)) with an on-policy actor-critic. The policy gradient is the score-function estimator ([policy gradient theorem](../math-cookbook.md#m-pg-theorem)),

$$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)\,\hat A_t\Big],$$

with $\hat A_t$ from GAE ([advantages](../math-cookbook.md#m-advantage-gae)) and a clipped surrogate or trust region ([PPO](../math-cookbook.md#m-ppo-clip), [TRPO](../math-cookbook.md#m-trust-region)). Actions are PD set-points ([actuators](../math-cookbook.md#m-pd-actuator)); terrain enters as heightmap samples ([heightmaps](../math-cookbook.md#m-terrain-lookup)); robustness is bought with [domain randomisation and teacher–student training](../math-cookbook.md#m-domain-rand) and [distillation](../math-cookbook.md#m-distillation); difficulty is scheduled by [curricula](../math-cookbook.md#m-curriculum); data are multiplied by [symmetry](../math-cookbook.md#m-symmetry). Two further devices recur without a cookbook entry: time-out bootstrapping of the critic ([legged_gym](../papers/legged-gym.md)) and per-reward-group critics with separately normalised advantages ([BeamDojo](../papers/beamdojo.md)).

## 5. Why sparse terrain is hard for PPO

The sparse-foothold papers agree on the symptoms; their numbers allow a diagnosis. In BeamDojo's hard stepping stones, PPO with the correct foothold reward reached 0.33 % success, and 0.67 % on beams. Zhang et al. could not train specialists from scratch in 10 000 iterations. AME's ablation with perfect perception but all terrains from the start failed to climb the curriculum. Extreme Parkour's and humanoid parkour's policies placed feet near edges until penalised. Four mechanisms explain this.

**Exploration under termination.** On continuous terrain, a poor action costs a little reward; on stepping stones, it ends the episode. With Gaussian action noise, the probability that a random perturbation sequence produces $k$ consecutive supported steps decays roughly geometrically in $k$, so successful trajectories, the only ones carrying information about good foot placement, are exponentially rare early in training. Every successful method changes the state distribution to compensate: curricula from easy terrain, BeamDojo's non-terminating flat-ground stage, Zhang et al.'s relaxed demotion, generalist pre-training.

**A step-function return.** The return as a function of foot position is nearly flat while the foot is on support and drops discontinuously at the edge. The score-function estimator does not differentiate the return; it correlates action noise with sampled outcomes. Where the return is flat, that correlation is zero in expectation and only noise remains; where it jumps, the estimate is informative only if the noise happens to straddle the edge. Hence the edge-hugging behaviour, and hence the hand-made dense surrogates: Extreme Parkour's 5 cm edge penalty, humanoid parkour's $-\ln\|d_x\|$ footstep reward, BeamDojo's area-proportional foothold penalty. Each injects, through the reward, a gradient the estimator cannot recover from the dynamics.

**Delayed, sparse credit.** BeamDojo notes that foothold reward arrives only after a full lift-and-land sub-process, and position-based formulations ([ANYmal parkour](../papers/anymal-parkour.md), [Zhang et al.](../papers/risky-terrains.md)) concentrate the task reward at the end of the episode. GAE must propagate credit backwards through a learned critic over hundreds of milliseconds to seconds; the variance of the likelihood-ratio estimator grows with that horizon ([GAE](../math-cookbook.md#m-advantage-gae)), and the critic itself is fitted to sparse, high-variance targets. BeamDojo's double critic is a direct response: separating the sparse foothold value from the dense locomotion value stops one from corrupting the other's normalised advantage, and makes foot trajectories adjust earlier in swing.

**Variance bought down by parallelism.** Massive parallelism addresses the estimator's variance with sample count, about $10^5$ samples per update, and symmetry augmentation multiplies that count further, which is why Zhang et al. found it the most important ingredient. But variance reduction by averaging scales as $1/\sqrt{N}$, and on sparse terrain the informative samples are a vanishing fraction of $N$. The result is days of GPU time (AME) and per-terrain specialists (Zhang et al.), not a failure in principle but an unfavourable constant.

The robots and perception systems are not the bottleneck: AME's attention shows that the foothold information is extractable from a heightmap, and BeamDojo's real robot places feet on 20 cm stones once a policy exists. The bottleneck is obtaining a learning signal that says *how* a small change in action shifts the foot relative to an edge.

## 6. Tensions and open problems

*Robustness versus precision.* Miki et al.'s noise model teaches the policy to distrust the map; sparse terrain requires treating edges as hard. AME and BeamDojo resolve this with two-stage training (exact terrain first, noise second), conceding that the training signal should see exact geometry even if the policy cannot. *Generalist versus specialist.* Zhang et al. could not train one policy across sparse terrains; AME could, at several days of compute. *Reward engineering.* Every sparse-terrain success adds a hand-designed geometric reward; failure cases (10 cm stones, 55 cm steps in BeamDojo) are attributed by the authors to the reward not covering them. *Perception.* Hardware results on sparse terrain often use motion capture and a known map (Zhang et al., AME's GR-1), and [elevation-map](../papers/elevation-mapping-gpu.md) drift of 5 cm can cause falls. *Fidelity.* Industrial GPU simulators are neither differentiable nor always faithful (Digit's closed chains), so they offer samples but not gradients.

## 7. How the group feeds other groups and TGI

From [g1](g1-rl-foundations.md) this group takes [PPO](../papers/ppo.md), [GAE](../papers/gae.md) and [TRPO](../papers/trpo.md) and turns them into an industrial recipe. It hands [g2](g2-world-models.md) its data source: [RWM](../papers/rwm.md), [SuperMimic](../papers/supermimic.md) and TGI fit world models to transitions from exactly these parallel simulators. Against [g4](g4-classical-control.md) it trades precision for robustness, and hybrids such as [DTC](../papers/dtc.md) sit between. Its humanoid strand meets the tracking line of [g7](g7-humanoid-tracking.md).

TGI ([g0](g0-anchor.md)) inherits almost the entire engineering stack of this group: Isaac Lab with thousands of parallel environments, PD set-points and the ANYdrive LSTM actuator model ([Hwangbo](../papers/hwangbo-anymal.md), [legged_gym](../papers/legged-gym.md)); heightmap terrain input and a success-guided terrain curriculum ([Lee](../papers/lee-challenging-terrain.md), [legged_gym](../papers/legged-gym.md)); the position-based ANYmal-D task over gaps, boxes and stepping stones ([ANYmal parkour](../papers/anymal-parkour.md), [Zhang](../papers/risky-terrains.md)); foothold-gated perception from [Watch Your Step](../papers/watch-your-step.md), the attention idea of [AME](../papers/attention-map-encoding.md) made explicit; and the G1 stepping-stone problem of [BeamDojo](../papers/beamdojo.md). What it replaces is the estimator. Following [SHAC](../papers/shac.md) and [SuperMimic](../papers/supermimic.md), TGI backpropagates a 32-step imagined return through a learned world model and differentiable terrain lookups, with a terminal critic ([reparameterisation](../math-cookbook.md#m-reparam), [BPTT](../math-cookbook.md#m-bptt), [short horizon](../math-cookbook.md#m-short-horizon)). Each mechanism of Section 5 has a counterpart: imagined rollouts restart from simulator states, so termination does not starve the signal; the exact terrain inside the model means foot-placement consequences are, away from edges, available as gradients rather than as hand-made rewards; the horizon is short, with the critic carrying longer credit; and where the pathwise gradient is untrustworthy, at imagined touchdowns on poorly supported terrain, TGI cuts it and defers to the critic, as BeamDojo deferred sparse foothold events to a separate critic ([contact gradients](../math-cookbook.md#m-contact-gradients)). Whether these first-order gradients are reliable through contact, and how much model bias they introduce ([compounding error](../math-cookbook.md#m-model-learning)), is the question examined in [g3](g3-analytic-gradients.md) and [g2](g2-world-models.md); this group establishes why the question is worth asking.

## Corpus additions

- [`risky-terrains`](../papers/risky-terrains.md) — Zhang, Rudin, Hoeller, Hutter, "Learning Agile Locomotion on Risky Terrains", IROS 2024, arXiv 2311.10484. Fills the gap between perceptive parkour and humanoid sparse footholds: the first end-to-end RL quadruped on stepping stones and beams, the RL baseline in AME and a terrain source for BeamDojo; it documents the exploration machinery PPO requires there.
- [`isaac-gym`](../papers/isaac-gym.md) — Makoviychuk et al., arXiv 2108.10470. The GPU substrate of the parallel paradigm; establishes that the simulator gives throughput but no gradients, the reason first-order successors use learned models.
- [`elevation-mapping-gpu`](../papers/elevation-mapping-gpu.md) — Miki et al., IROS 2022, arXiv 2204.12876. The deployment perception pipeline of the perceptive papers and of Watch Your Step; its error modes define the height-noise models.

Considered but not added: Mittal et al., symmetry for task-symmetric policies (ICRA 2024), covered by the cookbook's symmetry entry; Agarwal et al., egocentric-vision locomotion (CoRL 2022), whose recipe is described through Extreme Parkour.
